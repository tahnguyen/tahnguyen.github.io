### Context Statement

I am a fifth-year Ph.D. candidate in political science at Duke University, working in comparative politics. My research examines authoritarian politics, single-party regimes, and Southeast Asia, asking how institutional arrangements shape political behavior and why state capacity varies across autocracies. I use survey and field experiments, latent variable modeling, process tracing, and focus groups.

Most of my teaching since 2023 has been as a lab instructor and teaching assistant in graduate-level methods courses, from an R bootcamp through probability and regression to causal inference, including two summers at the Ralph Bunche Summer Institute. Outside methods, I was a TA for an undergraduate course in my research area, the political economy of Southeast Asia. I am committed to helping students go beyond running a model to judging whether it supports the claim they want to make, and to preparing them to use what they learn independently in their own research.


### Designing and Planning for Learning Activities [A1]

In most of the labs I have led, including POLSCI 630: Probability and Basic Regression and POLSCI 689L: Computational Methods in Social Sciences, I build the materials myself each week: annotated R scripts, handouts with worked examples, and, for some courses, slides. The sequence follows lecture, but I reorganize it around the steps students actually find hardest rather than the order in which concepts appear in the syllabus **[K2]**. My scripts pair extensive explanatory comments with deliberate gaps for students to fill in. That way students work through each step themselves, and the gaps become natural points for questions and discussion. I also treat the design as provisional. When informal mid-course comments showed that students found labs too fast and too full to digest, I cut the material per session substantially, kept fewer core tasks, and built in pauses for students to run code themselves before I moved on **[K3]**. Later feedback and in-session engagement suggested the stripped-down version worked better than trying to cover everything.

### Teaching and Supporting Student Learning [A2]

My lab sections typically have 15–20 graduate students and meet weekly to turn the week's statistical concepts into practice. I open each session with a quick check for understanding and time for questions, then re-explain the points from lecture that generated the most confusion **[K1]**. From there I work through a prepared slide deck and annotated script step by step, so students follow the reasoning behind each line rather than copying an answer **[K2]**. I write code live when a question calls for it, since a student's real error can be a useful demonstration. I end by walking through a complete working example, data included, so everyone leaves with a running version they can adapt on problem sets.

Because preparation varies widely in these courses, I also use structures that open up different ways to participate: pair debugging, a minute of writing before discussion, and asking for a specific kind of answer rather than a general one **[V2]**. In the R bootcamp for incoming students, taught in a flipped format, students work problems while the instructor and I circulate. Diagnosing an error there means judging quickly whether someone is stuck on syntax, on the statistical idea underneath it, or on reading the error message. Rather than fixing the code, I ask what the object is supposed to contain, so that students build a debugging process they can use on their own.


### Assessing and Giving Feedback for Learning [A3]

I have graded weekly problem sets and exams for multi-section methods courses, such as POLSCI 630: Probability and Basic Regression, using a shared rubric. Grading consistently across sections takes coordination. Our TA team met before and during grading to calibrate, worked through a set of answers together, agreed on how to award partial credit, and checked back in midway when unusual answers came up **[V5, K5]**. I also passed recurring errors back to the instructor so they could be addressed in lecture or in the following lab. A mistake that shows up across many submissions often says more about how the material landed than about the students.

In written comments, I aim for feedback students can act on rather than feedback that justifies a grade. I name the specific step where the reasoning or the code broke down, explain why the approach does not work, and give a concrete next action for revision. On paper drafts and replication exercises, I comment on the interpretation of results as much as on mechanics, especially on whether the model a student estimated actually supports the claim they are making. That judgment is what I most want students to take from these courses.


### Supporting and Guiding Learner [A4]

I hold weekly office hours for every course I support. Students usually arrive with code that will not run or a problem set they cannot start. I work from their own script rather than a clean example and ask them to narrate what they think each line does, which often locates the misunderstanding quickly. Some questions sit outside the course, such as a writing problem or a statistics question from their own research. For those, I help where I can and point them toward the appropriate campus resource rather than guessing. All of this depends on students being willing to say they are stuck. I learn names in the first few weeks and use them in section; in a required methods course that many students find intimidating, being known makes it easier to participate and ask questions **[V1]**. At the start of the semester, I also walk through course policy on collaboration and AI tools. I cover what kind of help with code is acceptable, what has to be their own work, and why the line falls where it does in courses that assess the reasoning behind a model, not just its output **[V4]**.

### Engaging in Professional Development as a Teacher [A5]

I am completing Duke's Certificate in College Teaching and earned the Certificate in Teaching Politics in 2025. Several practices from these programs are now part of my labs: active-learning structures, low-stakes checks for understanding, and stating plainly what an assignment is actually asking students to do **[V3]**. The check that opens each session came from this training. So did my response when mid-course feedback showed the labs were too full: fewer core tasks, pauses for students to run code, and the habit of asking them to try a step before I show the answer.

I have also mentored an incoming TA for a summer methods course. I shared my lab materials and R scripts, talked through how to structure a session, flagged the points in the syllabus where students predictably struggle, and discussed how to run a room where preparation varies widely. We debriefed after every session **[V5]**. Explaining my pacing decisions to someone else made me articulate choices I had been making by instinct, and I revised some of them in the process.

### Integrate and Reflect

What connects these practices is a view that learning methods means more than knowing the syntax: students also need to understand what a model is doing under the hood and how to interpret what it produces. That view shapes my materials, which leave gaps so students have to reason through each step rather than copy it. In lab, I walk through the reasoning behind each line; in office hours, I work from students' own code so they learn to find their own mistakes. It also shapes how I assess, asking students to interpret their models and explain their output, not just report it. I ask the same of my own teaching, treating student feedback as evidence to interpret: when it showed labs were too full, I changed them. Beyond any particular method, I want students to leave with the instinct to ask what their results actually show, and the persistence to keep going when the code fails, both of which independent research requires.

### Looking Ahead

I would like to design two courses of my own: (1) an undergraduate course on authoritarian politics, built on my research on single-party regimes and Southeast Asia; and (2) my own version of the first-year graduate course in probability and regression, for which I have led labs three times. I know where new students struggle, and I would build the course around what my labs already do: pairing each concept with its implementation in R and asking students to interpret a model, not just run it. I also want to learn how to design assessments that assume students have AI coding tools, asking them to explain and defend their code. After completing the Certificate in College Teaching, I plan to seek regular peer observation and keep sharing materials and approaches with other methods instructors, as I have with TAs in my department.
