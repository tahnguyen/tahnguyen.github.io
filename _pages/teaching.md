## Teaching Statement

_(The tags used in this teaching statement are drawn from the [Professional Standards
Framework for Teaching and Supporting Learning in Higher Education](https://advance-he.org/knowledge-hub/professional-standards-framework-teaching-and-supporting-learning-higher-education-0/)
developed by Advance HE.)_

### Designing and Planning for Learning Activities [A1]

For most of the methods courses I have supported, including POLSCI 630: Probability and Basic Regression and POLSCI 689L: Computational Methods in Social Sciences, I led the lab sections and built the materials myself each week: annotated R scripts, handouts with worked examples, and/or slides. The sequence follows lecture, but I reorganize it around the steps students actually find hardest rather than the order in which the concepts appear in the syllabus **[K2]**. I write scripts with extensive explanatory comments and with deliberate gaps for students to fill in rather than providing complete solutions, which creates opportunities for interaction and class discussion. I also keep the pacing and design flexible: when informal mid-course comments showed that students found labs too fast and too full to digest, I cut the amount of material per session substantially, kept fewer core tasks, and built in pauses where students ran code themselves before I moved on **[K3]**. Later feedback and in-session engagement suggested the stripped-down version worked better than trying to cover everything.

### Teaching and Supporting Student Learning [A2]

My lab sections often have 15–20 graduate students and meet weekly to turn the week's statistical concepts into practice. I leave time at the start of each session for questions and for re-explaining the points from lecture that generated the most confusion **[K1]**. From there I work through a prepared slide deck and annotated script step by step, so students follow the reasoning behind each line rather than copying an answer **[K2]**. I write code live when a question calls for it, since a student's real error can be a useful demonstration, and I leave time for students to run code themselves before I move on **[V2]**. I would then walk through a complete working example with data, so everyone leaves with a running version they can adapt on problem sets.

Additionally, I use structures that allow different types of participation: pair debugging, a minute of writing before discussion, and asking for a specific kind of answer rather than a general one **[V2]**. In the R bootcamp for incoming students, taught in a flipped format, students work problems while the instructor and I circulate. Diagnosing an error there means judging quickly whether someone is stuck on syntax, on the statistical idea underneath it, or on reading the error message. Rather than fixing the code, I ask what the object is supposed to contain, so that the debugging process is one they can repeat without me.

### Assessing and Giving Feedback for Learning [A3]

I have graded weekly problem sets and exams for multi-section methods courses, such as POLSCI 630: Probability and Basic Regression, using a shared rubric. Grading consistently across sections takes coordination: our TA team met before and during grading to discuss, worked through a set of answers together, agreed how to award partial credit, and checked back in midway when unusual answers came up **[V5, K5]**. I also passed recurring errors back to the instructor so they could be addressed in lecture or in the following lab, on the view that a mistake appearing repeatedly in student submissions could be a problem with how the material landed.

In written comments, I aim for feedback students can act on rather than feedback that justifies a grade: naming the specific step where the reasoning or the code broke down, explaining why the approach does not work, and giving a concrete next action for revision. On paper drafts and replication exercises, I comment on the interpretation of results as much as on mechanics. For example, I assess whether the claim a student is making is actually supported by the model they estimated, which is another skill these courses seek to build.

### Supporting and Guiding Learner [A4]

I hold weekly office hours for every courses I support. Students usually arrive with code that will not run or a problem set they cannot start. I work from their own script rather than a clean example and ask them to narrate what they think each line does, which often helps me locate the misunderstanding quickly. I also learn students' names in the first few weeks and use them in section; in a required methods course that many students find intimidating, being known makes it easier for them to participate and ask questions **[V1]**. When a question turns out to sit outside the course—a writing problem, or a statistics question from their own research—I help where I can and point them toward the appropriate campus resource rather than guessing. At the start of the semester I also walk through course policy on collaboration and on AI tools: what kind of help with code is acceptable, what has to be their own work, and why we also assess their reasoning for running a model rather than just the output **[V4]**.

### Engaging in Professional Development as a Teacher [A5]

I am enrolled in the College Teaching Certificate and have completed a graduate course in Teaching Politics. Specific practices learned from these courses have come into my lab sections: active-learning structures, low-stakes checks for understanding, and stating plainly what an assignment is actually asking students to do **[V3]**. The low-stakes check is the first five minutes of every session; the pauses I built into lab after mid-course feedback also came out of this, as did the habit of asking students to try something before I show them the answer.

I have also mentored an incoming TA for a summer methods course. I shared my lab materials and R scripts, talked through how to structure a session, flagged the points in the syllabus where students predictably struggle, and discussed how to run a room where preparation varies widely; we debriefed after every session **[V5]**. Explaining pacing decisions to someone else required me to articulate choices I had been making by instinct, and I revised some of them in the process.
